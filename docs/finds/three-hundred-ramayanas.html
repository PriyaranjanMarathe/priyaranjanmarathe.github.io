What changes when the story travels?

Ramanujan asks us to read each telling on its own terms. As you go, try tracking one thing: who gets to act, who gets the reader’s sympathy, or what a familiar episode now explains. The links below open the corresponding places in the 1991 edition.

  1. The opening: Hanuman and the rings

    Before offering any literary argument, Ramanujan answers a question about how many Ramayanas there are with another story. Why begin that way?1

    Read the opening ↗

  2. Vālmīki and Kampan: two Ahalyas

    Read the Sanskrit and Tamil tellings side by side. Notice what Ahalya knows and what Rama’s arrival does in each. A shared plot doesn’t guarantee a shared moral center.2

    Read the comparison ↗

  3. Jain tellings

    In the telling Ramanujan discusses, Lakshmana kills Ravana. Ask what changes when the teller separates the hero from the person who does the killing.3

    Read the Jain section ↗

  4. A Kannada oral telling

    Follow Sita’s birth and listen for the Kannada wordplay in her name. What does a performance let a storyteller do that a written synopsis would miss?4

    Read the oral-telling section ↗

  5. The Thai Ramakien

    Watch Hanuman. Which of his traits would surprise a reader who knows him mainly through devotional tellings?5

    Read the Thai example ↗

  6. Difference, translation, and listening

    Ramanujan distinguishes resemblance to a source, the imprint of a local setting, and the reuse of a story to say something new. These can coexist in the same retelling. Where would you put a song cycle written for radio?6

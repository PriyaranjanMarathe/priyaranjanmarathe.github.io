Post–World War II Truman → Harris Sixteen vice presidents, three fates

Ten of sixteen never became President

The vice presidency is often called a heartbeat away from the Oval Office — but since 1945, the heartbeat has usually been where the story ends. Of the 16 people who have held the office from Harry Truman through Kamala Harris, 10 never became President. And they failed in two very different ways.

The three fates

Every one of the sixteen lands in exactly one of three buckets. The largest group never got close enough to lose — their own party never handed them the nomination.

6

Became President

Made it to the top job — three by succession, three at the ballot box. This is the group the office is famous for producing, and it is the minority.

Harry Truman · Richard Nixon · Lyndon Johnson · Gerald Ford · George H. W. Bush · Joe Biden
4

Won the nomination, lost the election

Got the one thing the other six never did — a national ticket of their own — and then lost the general election. The most recent was just one cycle ago.

Hubert Humphrey, 1968 · Walter Mondale, 1984 · Al Gore, 2000 · Kamala Harris, 2024
6

Never even nominated

Never won their party's presidential nomination — some never seriously tried, some tried and stalled, and one resigned the vice presidency itself.

Alben Barkley · Spiro Agnew · Nelson Rockefeller · Dan Quayle · Dick Cheney · Mike Pence

Every VP who won a nomination and then lost the presidency is a Democrat — Humphrey, Mondale, Gore, and Harris. Republican VPs, by contrast, mostly never got the nomination at all: five of the six “never nominated” names are Republicans. (The one wrinkle in that clean line: Richard Nixon did lose a general election as the nominee, in 1960 — but he came back and won in 1968, so his final outcome is President.)

04

How the six got there

Even among the six who made it, half never won the office as a vice president running for promotion — they inherited it mid-term.

Inherited the office

Succession · 3 of 6
  • Harry Truman, 1945 Vice president for just 82 days before Franklin Roosevelt died; then won a term of his own in 1948.
  • Lyndon Johnson, 1963 Sworn in hours after John F. Kennedy's assassination; then won a landslide term of his own in 1964.
  • Gerald Ford, 1974 Succeeded Richard Nixon on his resignation — the only president in this set never elected to either national office. He lost his bid for a full term in 1976.

Won it at the ballot box

Election · 3 of 6
  • Richard Nixon, 1968 Lost the presidency in 1960, eight years after leaving the vice presidency he won it — the first “second act” on this list.
  • George H. W. Bush, 1988 The only one of the sixteen to move straight from the vice presidency into an elected presidency — the first sitting VP to do it since Martin Van Buren in 1836.
  • Joe Biden, 2020 Won four years after leaving office, after sitting out the 2016 cycle entirely. See the wrinkle below.

The direct handoff is the rarest path of all

Strip it down and only one of the sixteen — George H. W. Bush — was elected President directly from the vice presidency in the next election. Truman, Johnson, and Ford were already president when they won (or, in Ford's case, lost) their elections; Nixon waited eight years and Biden four. The “VP as heir apparent” story is mostly a story about succession and comebacks, not coronations.

05

The Biden wrinkle

Counting rule that matters

Not the nominee in 2016 — President anyway

If you counted only the immediate post-VP cycle, Joe Biden would look like a miss: in 2016 he didn't run at all, and Hillary Clinton took the Democratic nomination. But the count here is about final outcomes, not first cycles. In 2020 Biden won the nomination and the presidency — so he belongs with the six who made it, not the ten who didn't.

He's not the only delayed case, either — Nixon lost in 1960 and won in 1968. What makes Biden unique in the sixteen is that he skipped his first cycle altogether and still came back to win, which is exactly why he stays out of the “never became President” count.

How this was counted

Distinct individuals who served as vice president from the end of World War II onward, starting with Harry Truman (who took the office in January 1945) and ending with Kamala Harris. Each person is counted once, by final outcome, regardless of how many cycles they contested. JD Vance is excluded because his post-vice-presidency cycle has not yet occurred.

Outcomes: became President (by succession or election, at any point); nominated, lost (won a major-party presidential nomination and lost the general election, and never became President); never nominated (never won a major-party presidential nomination). Richard Nixon lost the 1960 general election as the Republican nominee before winning in 1968; he is counted by his final outcome, as President.

§ 1

What each party actually did

The split personality of the night: Democrats won the statewide offices, the U.S. Senate race and most U.S. House seats, while several conservative citizen initiatives passed anyway. Voters separated the people from the policies.

Democrats

Won the people — offices, Congress, legislature
  • Swept all four statewide offices — Governor, Attorney General, Secretary of State, Treasurer — each an open seat and a Democratic hold, by 17 to 27 points.
  • Re-elected Sen. John Hickenlooper by 24.7 points.
  • Flipped two Republican U.S. House seats — CD-5 (Colorado Springs, first Democrat ever to win it) and CD-8 — and lead in a third, CD-4, where Lauren Boebert trails.
  • On Nov. 5 leads: 7 gains, no losses: +1 in the Senate (24–11) and +6 in the House (49–16) on current leads — two-thirds supermajorities in both chambers.
  • Held their existing board seats: all three CU Regent seats; State Board of Education retains a Democratic majority: 5–4 if Wright wins CD-3, 6–3 if Clementi wins.
  • On measures: the graduated income tax (Amendment 87) and K-12 funding (Proposition NN) are passing; the 4.4% tax cap (Prop. 136) is failing.

Republicans

Lost the offices — but won several policy fights
  • Lost all four statewide offices and the Senate race by roughly 25 points.
  • Two House incumbents defeated (Jeff Crank in CD-5, Gabe Evans in CD-8); a third, Lauren Boebert in CD-4, trails in an uncalled race. AP called CD-3 for Jeff Hurd on Nov. 6.
  • Were on course to lose 7 legislative seats and with them the numbers needed to block veto overrides and referred amendments in either chamber.
  • Four conservative propositions were passing: Prop. 132 (fentanyl penalties), Prop. 133 (life without parole for trafficking a minor — the biggest Yes of the night at 62.15%), Prop. 134 (school sports by biological sex) and Prop. 135 (barring sex-altering surgery on minors).
  • But all six Republican-backed constitutional amendments were below their passing threshold — Amendments 81–86 — and the income-tax cap, Prop. 136, was trailing while Amendment 87’s graduated tax was leading. Uncalled measures remain provisional.

§ 8

Still open — and what happens next

The cited Nov. 5 compilation showed no statewide, federal or legislative race inside the automatic-recount margin (a lead of 0.5% or less of the winner's vote total). Two local measures identified in the cited snapshot were within the margin: Jefferson County Schools Ballot Issue 5A, where No leads Yes by 3 votes (150,894 to 150,891), and Bennett Fire District Ballot Issue 7F, where Yes leads by 5 votes (1,857 to 1,852). Both recounts can only be ordered after county canvass. Reports that the Boebert or Evans campaigns would seek recounts rest on single secondary sources and were not confirmed in Colorado outlets — treat them as talk, not filings.

§ 9

Questions & answers

The parts of these results that don't explain themselves.

Amendment 85 has more Yes than No votes. Why is it marked failing?
Because of the 55% rule. Since 2016, any amendment that adds language to the Colorado constitution needs 55% Yes, not a bare majority. Amendment 85 (plain-language ballot questions) has 51.17% Yes to 48.83% No in this snapshot — a lead of 59,445 votes — but is 3.83 points below the threshold. That is why it is marked failing rather than passing. It was uncalled in the original snapshot, and remaining ballots can change the totals.
Then why did Amendment 87 — also a constitutional amendment — need only a majority?
The 55% rule applies only to amendments that add language. The Secretary of State lists Amendment 87 as requiring a simple majority because it works by repealing the constitution's single-rate income-tax requirement rather than adding new language. In this snapshot, Amendment 87 is above its threshold at 51.20%, while the rival tax cap, Proposition 136, is below its threshold at 47.37%. Both were uncalled; these are not certified outcomes.
Which passing measure had the smallest Yes–No vote gap?
Amendment 87, the graduated income tax: Yes leads by 60,866 votes (51.20%–48.80%). Next is Proposition 137 (conservation funding from sporting-goods tax revenue) at 74,054 votes, then Proposition 135 at 107,499. Amendment 85 has a smaller Yes–No gap (59,445) but is below its separate 55% passing threshold, so a Yes–No gap alone does not rank how close a constitutional measure is to passing. At the other extreme, Proposition 133 drew the highest Yes share of the night — 62.15% — and Amendment 84 (mail-ballot ID numbers) the lowest, 36.82%.
Is Lauren Boebert's race over?
Not officially. AP had not called CD-4 as of Nov. 6: Eileen Laubacher (D) leads Boebert 184,103 (48.75%) to 181,079 (47.95%) with about 97% of the vote counted. The 3,024-vote lead is about three times the automatic-recount threshold (~920 votes), so no recount is triggered unless the margin collapses as cure and overseas ballots are counted through Nov. 12. Boebert had not conceded in any Colorado source indexed as of Nov. 6.
How did Republicans lose the statewide offices while four conservative measures were passing?
Because measures aren't candidates. Props. 132–135 were citizen initiatives decided on their own merits, and each drew majority support — 52–62% Yes — from an electorate that simultaneously voted 59–61% Democratic for governor and senator. The pattern has a limit, though: when conservative ideas were packaged as constitutional amendments needing 55%, all six were below that threshold in the snapshot, and the tax cap (Prop. 136) was trailing too. Uncalled measures remain provisional. Voters picked Democratic people and a mixed bag of policies.
What does a legislative supermajority actually let Democrats do?
Two things mainly: override a governor's veto and refer constitutional amendments to the ballot, both of which take two-thirds of each chamber. In the Senate, 24 of 35 meets the two-thirds threshold; the Nov. 5 projection includes the then-uncalled Democratic leads in SD-11 and SD-30. In the House, 49 of 65 clears the 44-seat bar with room to spare. Note the practical catch: Governor-elect Weiser is himself a Democrat, so veto overrides matter less than the amendment-referral power — which provides a legislative route to put future constitutional amendments before voters.
When are these results final?
Statewide certification is scheduled for November 30. Cure materials and eligible military/overseas ballots are due Nov. 12; county canvasses must finish by Nov. 25. Mandatory recounts must be ordered by Nov. 27 and completed by Dec. 4; requested recounts must finish by Dec. 8. The figures here are unofficial. CD-3 was called by AP on Nov. 6, while CD-4 remained uncalled. Other call labels retain the dated source snapshot rather than claiming to be a live tally.
Which local measures were within recount margins in this snapshot?
The cited snapshot identified two local measures within the automatic-recount margin: Jeffco Schools Issue 5A, trailing by 3 votes out of ~301,000 cast, and Bennett Fire Issue 7F, leading by 5 votes out of ~3,700. The closest legislative race, HD-21 at 240 votes, sounds tiny but is about 2.23% of the leader’s total — more than four times the 0.5% threshold. A losing candidate can also request and pay for a recount at a wider margin, but no formal request was verified in the sources as of Nov. 6.

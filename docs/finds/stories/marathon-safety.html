Run for your life —
what the data says about risk.

According to local reports citing police, Kommineni Gopi, a 36-year-old software engineer with IBM, died minutes after finishing the Hyderabad Hi-Tech Full Marathon on Sunday, 1 November 2026 — he had crossed the line at Gachibowli Stadium, collected his finisher's medal, and collapsed walking toward breakfast. His death is a tragedy, and it is also the kind of headline that makes millions of runners quietly wonder whether the sport they love is dangerous. So we put the headline next to a large US registry addressing race-associated cardiac arrest — the RACER registry's analysis of 29.3 million race finishes (Kim et al., JAMA 2025) — and this is what it actually shows.

The short version: race-associated cardiac death is rare, but the risk is not zero. In two US registry periods, its rate fell from 0.39 to 0.20 per 100,000 race finishes. Separately, regular running is associated with longer life. That does not establish that running a marathon is safer than not entering one.

What happened

Sunday morning in Hyderabad

Kommineni Gopi, 36, was a software engineer with IBM and, in the words of those close to him, a fitness enthusiast. Originally from Ravinuthala village in Khammam district, he had settled in Hyderabad with his family — at Alkapur Colony in Manikonda. On 1 November 2026 he ran the 8th edition of the Hyderabad Hi-Tech Full Marathon at Gachibowli Stadium. He did the hardest part. Then, minutes after it was over, he died.

A reported collapse is not a confirmed diagnosis. Contemporary news reports say Gopi collapsed after finishing. They do not establish his underlying medical condition. Cardiac arrest means the heart has stopped pumping effectively; a heart attack is one possible cause, not a synonym. The registry below tracks cardiac arrests during races and up to an hour afterwards.

His death has shaken Hyderabad's running community and put medical screening and emergency support at mass-participation races back in focus. Our thoughts are with his family, his friends, and everyone who ran alongside him on Sunday. His story deserves care; population data can add context without explaining an individual death.

The denominator

One death is a tragedy. 29.3 million finishes are the context.

News reports count deaths one at a time, with no denominator. The Race Associated Cardiac Event Registry (RACER) counts both. In March 2025, Jonathan H. Kim of Emory University and colleagues published a new registry analysis in JAMA: cardiac arrests identified through systematic surveillance at US marathons and half-marathons from 2010 to 2023, set against every finisher.

29.3M
Race finishes — 29,311,597 marathon and half-marathon finishers in the US, 2010–2023. This counts finishes, not unique people; one runner may enter many races.
176
Cardiac arrests in all those races over 14 years — about 1 in every 166,500 finishes.
66%
Survived. 117 of 176 people with a recorded cardiac arrest survived to hospital discharge (66%), compared with 29% in the earlier registry.

In rate terms: 0.60 cardiac arrests per 100,000 race finishes, and 0.20 cardiac deaths per 100,000 — one death per roughly 500,000 finishes. To feel what that means at the scale of a single race, use the explorer below. Every figure comes from Kim et al., JAMA 2025.

The two registry reports found similar overall cardiac arrest rates: 0.54 and 0.60 per 100,000 race finishes. The cardiac death rate fell from 0.39 to 0.20 per 100,000. Why did survival improve? The authors point to rapid CPR, AED access and emergency planning as plausible contributors. Detailed clinical and emergency-response information was available for only 36 cases, so the registry cannot prove how much each change caused the improvement. The practical lesson is to prepare for a fast response along the whole course and at the finish.

The pattern inside the rarity

Rare everywhere — but not evenly rare

A rate of 0.60 per 100,000 is an average over very different runners and very different miles. In the 2010–2023 report:

~6×

Men vs women: 1.12 arrests per 100,000 male finishes vs 0.19 per 100,000 female finishes. Sex was known in 146 of 176 cases: 127 men and 19 women; 30 were unknown. The published rates account for missing data using imputation.

~2.2×

Marathon vs half: 1.04 per 100,000 in the full marathon vs 0.47 in the half. The observed marathon rate was about 2.2 times the half-marathon rate. This does not isolate the effect of distance from differences between runners or events.

40%

Coronary artery disease was the most common identified cause: 27 of 67 cases with an established cause, about 40%. A cause was known for only 67 of the 176 arrests, so this is not 40% of all cases.

Race-associated cardiac arrests often cluster in the final quarter and near the finish. This supports strong medical and AED coverage there, alongside a rehearsed response throughout the course. These data do not establish that a finishing sprint or an abrupt stop causes the pattern.

The exercise paradox

Yes, the risk spikes during a run. That's only half the sentence.

The exercise paradox is that vigorous exertion can briefly raise cardiac-event risk, especially in susceptible people, while regular physical activity supports long-term cardiovascular health. Siscovick and colleagues (NEJM, 1984) studied men without known heart disease. Their historical estimates were:

56×

Relative risk of cardiac arrest during vigorous exercise for men who were usually sedentary compared with the same group at other times.

5×

The same during-exercise spike for men who exercised vigorously habitually. The exertion-related relative risk was much smaller in this group, but this was not a randomized training trial.

0.4×

The habitual exercisers' overall risk of cardiac arrest — during exercise and at rest combined — was just 40% of the sedentary men's.

These historical relative risks are not an individual's chance of dying, and they cannot diagnose what happened to Gopi. A race can trigger an event in someone with an underlying condition, but neither fitness nor a lack of symptoms guarantees protection. Regular activity, gradual preparation and attention to warning symptoms are more useful takeaways than treating a marathon as a medical stress test.

The other side of the ledger

What long-term running studies show

Not running is not the same as being inactive: walking, cycling and other activities also count. Lee and colleagues (Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 2014) followed 55,137 adults for an average of 15 years. After adjustment for measured differences, runners had these associations compared with non-runners:

And it isn't one study standing alone. Pedisic and colleagues' meta-analysis (British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2020) pooled 14 reports from six prospective cohorts with 232,149 people and found running associated with 27% lower all-cause mortality, 30% lower cardiovascular mortality, and 23% lower cancer mortality than not running. These observational studies cannot rule out residual confounding or establish the extra benefit of marathon racing. In Lee's data, the association appeared at much smaller amounts than marathon training: Running even 5–10 minutes a day, at a slow pace under 6 mph, was associated with meaningfully lower risk. There is no need to enter a marathon to be physically active.

For historical context, Dan Tunstall Pedoe reported about one cardiac death per 80,000 completed runs across roughly 650,000 London Marathon finishes in 1981–2006. That single-marathon series is not directly comparable with the later US registry, which is mostly half-marathon finishes. Within the US reports, survival after cardiac arrest improved; the newer survival figure refers to cardiac arrest, not to every type of race emergency.

What the data asks of you

Not "don't run." Run like the numbers are watching.

Low population risk is not a reason for complacency, and it does not determine any one person's risk. The evidence points to a short, concrete list:

  • 01Build gradually; arrive trainedThe during-exercise spike is largest in people unaccustomed to vigorous effort (56× in Siscovick's sedentary group vs 5× in the habitually active). Build exercise intensity and duration gradually; avoid a sudden jump into strenuous exercise.
  • 02Know your family history — and say it out loudEarly heart disease in close relatives, unexplained fainting, or a family death at a young age are reasons to talk to a doctor before a first marathon, not after a scare.
  • 03Treat symptoms as data, not weaknessStop exercising and seek medical attention for chest pain or pressure, unusual breathlessness, dizziness, fainting or concerning palpitations. Severe or persistent symptoms call for emergency help. Do not train or race through them.
  • 04Respect the last quarter — and the finishArrests often occur late in a race, but warning symptoms matter at any point. Stop and alert the medical team rather than merely slowing down or trying to reach the line.
  • 05Don't race sick, and don't debut at 42.2 km on a dareDo not race with a fever or when acutely unwell; seek medical advice when needed. Choose a distance suited to your training. The lower average arrest rate in half-marathons is not a personal safety guarantee.
  • 06Learn CPR. You are the intervention.If someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally, summon emergency help, start CPR and get an AED. Follow its prompts. CPR/AED training can help you respond; it cannot guarantee survival.

A note on screening: Discuss symptoms, existing conditions and a family history of premature heart disease or sudden death with a qualified clinician, especially before a major increase in exercise. Screening cannot detect every condition or guarantee safety; the right assessment depends on the individual. Organizers also need a rehearsed emergency plan, trained responders and accessible AEDs.

The verdict

Keep running in perspective. Take symptoms seriously.

Kommineni Gopi's death is a human loss, not a statistic to explain away. The population evidence still matters: race-associated cardiac death was rare in this US registry, and most people with a recorded arrest survived. Separately, habitual running was associated with lower long-term mortality. Neither result tells us why he died or guarantees that a marathon is safe for a particular runner.

Enjoy physical activity, build gradually, pay attention to symptoms and choose events with sound medical preparation. You do not need 42.2 kilometres to gain from an active life. For personal risk or medical clearance, talk with a qualified clinician.

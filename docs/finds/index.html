The trio set out from Santa Monica Beach in Los Angeles on August 1 and reached the Washington Monument in Washington, DC, on September 21. They averaged about 65 miles a day, crossing 14 states and tackling more than 100,000 feet of climbing. Part of the journey followed historic Route 66 before the cyclists continued east toward Washington. The ride brought extreme heat, thunderstorms and cold weather, along with stretches of highway without shoulders and heavy truck traffic. Rawat and Samir Palnitkar are graduates of IIT Kanpur and have been friends for 40 years. Palnitkar is a dietitian who now teaches at a special needs school. This was her first long cycle ride. The three have trekked together in Nepal, India, Patagonia, Ecuador, France, Italy, Switzerland and Africa. Yes, people frequently died from eating sandwiches—and the components that went into them—before the FDA and other food regulations were established. [1, 2] In the 19th and early 20th centuries, before the landmark Pure Food and Drugs Act of 1906 created federal oversight, the American food supply was virtually lawless. While the term "sandwich" became popular in the late 1800s as a quick lunch for the working class, sitting down to eat one meant risking exposure to lethal bacteria, rotting meat, and toxic industrial chemicals. [2, 3, 4] Here is how a routine sandwich could have killed someone before regulations: ## 💀 The Bread: Toxic Fillers and Fungal Poisoning To maximize profits, bakeries frequently stretched flour with cheaper, dangerous additives. [2, 5] * Chemical Adulterants: It was common to find bread whitened with alum (aluminum sulfate) or chalk, and bulked up with plaster of Paris, clay, or even sawdust. * Ergot Poisoning: Unregulated grain harvesting meant that rye and wheat were frequently contaminated with Claviceps purpurea, a toxic fungus. Eating bread made from this grain caused ergotism (St. Anthony’s Fire), which led to gangrene, severe hallucinations, convulsions, and death. ## 🥩 The Meat: "Tubercular Beef" and Formaldehyde Before federal meat inspections, there were no laws preventing companies from processing diseased animals. [2, 6] * The Preservatives: Because commercial refrigeration did not exist, meat packers kept decomposing flesh looking "fresh" by treating it with borax, lethal doses of sodium sulfite, and formaldehyde (embalming fluid) to mask the stench of rot. [2, 3, 5] * Diseased Livestock: As Upton Sinclair famously exposed in his 1906 novel The Jungle, scrap meat poured into sausages routinely included rats, poisoned rat bait, and meat from animals that died of tuberculosis or cholera. ## 🧀 The Cheese and Condiments: "Swill Milk" and Lead If someone preferred a cheese or egg sandwich, the danger was just as high. [2] * Swill Dairy Products: In urban areas, dairy cows were kept in filthy, unventilated stables attached to whiskey distilleries and fed boiling alcohol waste ("slop"). This produced a thin, bluish, bacteria-ridden liquid known as swill milk. To make it look like thick, rich cream for cheese or butter, manufacturers added chalk, plaster, starch, and pureed calf brains. Swill dairy killed thousands of people—primarily children—every year. [2, 7, 8, 9, 10] * Poisonous Colors: Pickles or mustard added to a sandwich were often brightly colored using industrial dyes containing copper sulfate, lead, and arsenic. [2, 11] ## 🔬 The Turning Point The scale of foodborne fatalities finally led to change through the work of Dr. Harvey Washington Wiley and his famous "Poison Squad" experiments in the early 1900s, where human volunteers ingested these common food additives to document their toxic effects. Their findings horrified the public and directly prompted the creation of the agency that would become the FDA, turning the simple act of eating a sandwich from a gamble with death into a safe, everyday routine.

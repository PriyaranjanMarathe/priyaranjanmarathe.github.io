An armchair impact analysis · Denver metro

Lawns, data centers, and the resources they actually use

Outdoor landscaping consumes a great deal of Denver's treated water. A new data center in Parker will consume a great deal of electricity. Both facts are true—and putting them in the same argument can still lead us badly astray.

August 10, 2026About 10 minutesJump to the answer
The answer first

This is not a contest between lawns and computers

The comparison began with an appealing intuition: if data centers are criticized for consuming resources, perhaps Denver's lawns deserve equal or greater scrutiny. The numbers reveal a more useful conclusion.

Landscaping is primarily a water-policy question. The Parker data center is primarily an electricity, grid-cost, and carbon question. One does not cancel the other because gallons and gigawatt-hours solve different local problems.

What the evidence supports

Reducing nonfunctional irrigated turf is a large, actionable conservation lever. Parker's published design is a material new electrical load.

What it does not support

Parker is not credibly described as a cooling-water guzzler, and 22.5 MW does not by itself prove the county needs a nuclear reactor.
The method

Numbers, denominators, counterfactuals

David MacKay organized Sustainable Energy—without the hot air around “numbers, not adjectives”: put demand and supply on a balance sheet, state assumptions, and ask whether a proposed solution is large enough to matter.

Andy Masley's data-center essays add two useful cautions: a visible facility is not the same as a large net impact, and a national category claim is not proof of a local harm. His conclusions are arguments to test, not authority to borrow. The useful habits are to find the denominator, name the counterfactual, and trace the mechanism of harm.

1 · Magnitude

How large?

Annualize published figures without disguising capacity as consumption.
2 · Mechanism

Which resource?

Separate water, power, carbon, land, noise, and financial exposure.
3 · Decision

What changes?

Find the contract, permit, tariff, or conservation program that controls the outcome.
Where the common balance sheet stops: gallons and gigawatt-hours should not be collapsed into one “environmental badness” score. They belong on separate local balance sheets.

Denver Water's audited report records 45.974 billion gallons for metered general customers and 2.115 billion gallons for public authorities in 2024. Denver Water separately says 47% of retail treated-water use is single-family and the average single-family customer uses 50% outdoors.

48.089B gallons × 47% × 50% = 11.300B gallons/year

This is an order-of-magnitude estimate of single-family outdoor use. It includes trees, shrubs, gardens, and turf. It is not a meter reading for lawns, and it covers Denver Water's service area rather than the whole metro.

The qualification does not make the number unimportant. Denver Water identifies outdoor use as a conservation opportunity and subsidizes turf removal. The careful target is nonfunctional irrigated turf, not every green space: athletic fields, parks, shade landscapes, and trees provide benefits that decorative median grass may not.

The denominator is 45.9744B gallons to metered general customers plus 2.114848B gallons to public authorities. Applying system-wide shares creates an estimate, not direct measurement. Wholesale/resale water is excluded.

The operator publishes 22.5 MW of critical IT capacity and a power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.3. If every published IT megawatt ran continuously for a year, total facility demand would be 29.25 MW and annual electricity use about 256 GWh.

22.5 MW × 1.3 PUE × 8,760 hours = 256.23 GWh/year
This is a ceiling-style scenario, not a forecast. Actual use depends on occupancy, customer demand, maintenance, and operating conditions. The public record reviewed here does not provide meter data.

The Town of Parker says the facility will not use water to cool its equipment and will use closed-loop systems; ordinary interior water use remains. For this project, the direct local resource question therefore shifts from cooling water to electricity.

The most important figure remains missing: emissions and bill impacts cannot be calculated from 22.5 MW alone. That requires actual hourly load, the resources serving incremental demand, grid upgrades, contract terms, and cost allocation.

Audit the scenario

22.5 MW IT × 1.3 = 29.25 MW total demand at full published IT load. Multiplying by 8,760 hours gives 256,230 MWh. Calling this actual annual consumption would be misleading.

Masley's salience warning matters: a data center is visible, new, and concentrated, while many ordinary upstream impacts are dispersed. But concentration can still create genuine local grid, noise, air-quality, or fiscal problems. Local evidence—not a national average—decides the case.

Who pays?

The strongest civic question is contractual

There is no evidence here that Parker turns a $100 household bill into $120. That should not be presented as a forecast. The underlying risk is legitimate: infrastructure built for one customer can become everybody else's burden if costs are misallocated or the customer leaves.

Colorado's Xcel proceeding offers a useful menu—long contracts, project-specific infrastructure charges, minimum payments, and early-termination protection. Those proposals do not govern Parker, which CORE serves. They show how “the customer pays” can become enforceable rather than rhetorical.

  1. Show the incremental cost. What generation, transmission, substation, and interconnection work exists only because of this load?
  2. Show the allocation. Which costs stay with the customer, and which enter the shared rate base?
  3. Show the exit protection. What term, payment floor, exit charge, collateral, or guarantee applies if demand disappears?
  4. Show the alternatives. Compare flexibility, transmission, storage, renewables, gas, geothermal, and nuclear on common assumptions.
And the SMR?

Study the problem before naming the machine

Douglas County's material describes exploration of small modular reactors and microreactors for broader resilience and economic development. It does not establish that Parker's 22.5 MW project requires one.

A technology-first question—“Should the county build an SMR?”—invites a fight over identity. A MacKay-style question is harder and better:

What portfolio can serve plausible new loads reliably, on time, with acceptable carbon, water, land, and financial risk—and who bears each risk?

Nuclear can sit in that comparison. It should not be smuggled into the premise.

Bottom line

Follow each resource to a decision

On water

Keep removing nonfunctional, water-intensive turf. The scale is large, the mechanism is clear, and an existing program provides a lever.

On Parker

Scrutinize power supply, marginal emissions, grid upgrades, cost allocation, exit protection, noise, and backup generation—not a generic cooling-water claim.

The correct story is not “lawns are worse than data centers.” Environmental arguments improve when we stop sorting visible things into good and bad categories and start tracing each resource, at the margin, to a decision.

What would change this analysis? Actual operating data; a public service agreement; documented grid upgrades; an hourly supply mix; credible noise or air-quality measurements; or a county study comparing energy options on common assumptions.

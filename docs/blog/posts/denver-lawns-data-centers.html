Denver lawns and data centers:
a quantitative check

The useful question is not “which one is bad?” It is: which local resource is each activity actually stressing, at what scale?

About 16 minutes TL;DR: about 2 minutes Jump to TL;DR

TL;DR — what a neighbor should know before a meeting

Water:
Outdoor residential water use is a very large local conservation lever. Denver Water's published data imply roughly 11.3 billion gallons/year of single-family outdoor use across its service area. That is landscaping broadly—not lawn-only.
Parker cooling:
The Town says the Parker data center does not use water to cool its equipment; water is for ordinary interior uses. So the strongest local resource question for this project is electricity, not cooling water.
Electric bills:
There is no evidence that a $100 household bill becomes $120 because of Parker. The Town says CORE has represented that this project will pay for its own infrastructure and energy and will not raise other CORE members' rates. Residents should ask to see the cost-allocation logic behind that assurance.
SMR:
Parker's 22.5 MW load does not by itself prove Douglas County needs an SMR. The County is still exploring whether SMRs or microreactors could make sense for broader future energy resilience and economic-development loads.
Best meeting question: “For each new large electrical load, what new infrastructure or generation is required, who pays for it, what happens if the customer leaves, and what alternatives were compared before choosing a technology?”

Bottom line

Important correction after re-checking the sources: The Parker data center should not be described as a water-guzzling facility. Independent local business reporting says the Parker facility uses water-free cooling. The operator publishes a 22.5 MW critical IT load and a 1.3 PUE, and describes its newest high-density designs as using air-cooled chillers with closed cooling loops and zero water usage effectiveness (WUE) for cooling. The defensible claim is therefore near-zero on-site cooling-water consumption — not literally zero water everywhere in the building or in the electricity supply chain.

“Outdoor water” and “irrigation” are not synonymous with lawn water: they also include trees, gardens, shrubs and other landscaping. The figures are therefore a landscaping proxy, not an exact turf-only measurement.

What the water numbers actually say

The cleanest public comparison is for the Denver Water service area, which serves about 1.5 million people across Denver and surrounding suburbs. This is not a measurement of every lawn in the entire Denver metro.

Denver Water's 2024 audited report records 48.089 billion gallons of retail treated-water sales before wholesale/resale accounts: 45.974 billion gallons to metered general customers plus 2.115 billion gallons to public authorities. Denver Water's current key-facts page says 47% of retail treated-water use is single-family and the average single-family customer uses 50% outdoors.

System-level outdoor-use estimate:
48.089B gallons × 47% × 50% ≈ 11.30 billion gallons/year.

That is not “11.3 billion gallons of lawn water.” It is a reasonable estimate of single-family outdoor water, which includes turf, trees, shrubs, gardens and other landscaping. Denver Water also reports irrigation-only accounts separately. The public data do not let us isolate turf precisely enough to label all of this lawn consumption.

A more defensible civic target than “lawns are useless” is nonfunctional irrigated turf. Denver Water itself says water-thirsty Kentucky bluegrass often is a household's biggest water user and operates a turf-removal program. During the 2026 drought, its efficient outdoor target has been tightened from the normal 12 gallons per square foot to 8 gallons per square foot.

So where should civic attention go?

Water scarcity

Landscaping is an obvious, large, locally controllable lever. Denver Water explicitly says about half of single-family residential water use is outdoors, and the city is already targeting nonfunctional turf.

Grid / climate

Data centers are the much bigger new electricity question. A 22.5-MW facility can operate around the clock; 160-MW-scale campuses are a different order of magnitude from ordinary commercial buildings.

Policy

It is not rational to choose only one issue. Turf reform addresses water; data-center rules address grid cost, generation, emissions, noise and backup power. They are mostly different bottlenecks.

Claims I would — and would not — make to neighbors

Supported: Denver-area data centers can be extremely electricity-intensive. Parker has 22.5 MW of critical IT capacity and a published PUE of 1.3. Large additional capacity is planned elsewhere in the metro.

Supported: Outdoor residential water use is a very large Denver-area conservation lever. A reasonable Denver Water service-area estimate from its published 2024 data is about 11.3 billion gallons/year of single-family outdoor use.

Supported, with careful wording: Parker is designed to avoid the large on-site cooling-water consumption associated with evaporative cooling. Independent reporting calls its cooling “water-free.”

Not supported: “Parker uses literally zero water.” Bathrooms, cleaning and other building uses can remain, and electricity generation can have an upstream water footprint.

Not supported: “Denver lawns use 11.3 billion gallons.” The 11.3B estimate is all single-family outdoor use, not turf alone.

Not supported: “All data centers are water guzzlers.” Cooling technology varies enormously between facilities.

Not supported: “The Parker project was built because of the Biden infrastructure law.” I found no project-specific grant, financing or official document tying this facility to the 2021 federal infrastructure law.

What can be said with high confidence

  • Denver landscaping consumes billions of gallons of treated water in a typical year.
  • Nonfunctional turf is a legitimate conservation target and is already being reduced by local water agencies.
  • The Parker data center is highly electricity-intensive but is not designed to consume water for cooling.
  • Large additional data-center electrical capacity is being built/planned around the Denver metro.
  • “MW of capacity” is not the same as actual annual electricity consumed.
  • There is no honest single number that lets us call either “worse for the environment” without choosing an impact: water, carbon, land, noise, grid cost, habitat, or something else.

Going to a public meeting? Ask these questions first

This is the practical TL;DR. The Parker project is a 22.5 MW facility served by CORE Electric Cooperative, not Xcel Energy. The Town of Parker says CORE has represented that this specific project will pay for its own infrastructure and energy and will not increase rates for other CORE members. That claim is important enough to verify in the actual service agreement or cost-of-service documentation if it becomes public.

Do not start with “Will my $100 electric bill become $120?” There is no public evidence supporting a 20% Parker-data-center increase. Start with: “Show us the cost allocation. Which project-driven costs are assigned to the large customer, and which can reach existing members?”
Question to askWhy it mattersWhat a strong answer looks like
Will existing households pay any of the cost of serving this data center? A large load can require transmission, substations, interconnection work and generation. The key affordability question is cost allocation, not simply how many megawatts the customer uses. A transparent cost-of-service explanation identifying project-specific costs and showing whether they are directly assigned to the large customer. Parker says CORE has stated the Parker project will pay for its own infrastructure and energy.
What happens if the data center reserves capacity and later uses less power, closes, or leaves? Infrastructure may be built before demand fully materializes. If the customer disappears, somebody can be left paying for underused assets. Long-term service commitments, minimum payments, credit/security requirements and meaningful early-termination charges. These are not exotic ideas: Xcel's pending Colorado large-load proposal uses a 15-year minimum term, minimum demand charges and exit protections.
Does Parker need new generation for this 22.5 MW facility? It is easy to jump from “large electricity user” to “build a power plant.” But Parker says the facility will be supplied from an existing CORE substation directly adjacent to the site. A utility load study showing available capacity, expected peak demand, required upgrades and the marginal generation/resource need. Do not infer the need for a new reactor from the data-center nameplate alone.
Does this project prove Douglas County needs an SMR? No. The county's SMR work is broader: it is exploring whether SMRs or microreactors could support future resilience and economic-development loads including data centers, aerospace and advanced manufacturing. A technology-neutral study that first defines future load scenarios and then compares alternatives on cost, schedule, reliability, emissions, land/water needs and financial risk.
If more large data centers arrive, what are the supply options? The answer need not be one technology. Compare grid/transmission expansion, contracted generation, renewables plus storage, geothermal where feasible, gas with its emissions/local-pollution consequences, nuclear/SMR, demand flexibility, and combinations. State what each option can realistically deliver and by when.
Could a large customer bring or contract for its own power? This can reduce the amount of generation cost socialized through ordinary rates, though interconnection and reliability issues remain. A clearly defined self-supply or power-purchase arrangement plus rules for backup/grid service and infrastructure costs. Federal regulators are actively considering frameworks that let very large loads bring their own supply while protecting reliability and consumers.
Can the data center reduce demand when the grid is stressed? Some computing loads can be shifted in time or between locations; others require continuous service. Flexibility can reduce the amount of infrastructure needed for rare peaks. A demand-response or flexible-connection agreement specifying how many MW can actually be curtailed, for how long, how often, and what happens to backup-generator emissions when curtailment occurs.
What financial protection exists against stranded assets? This is the “AI boom changes direction” problem. Up-front contributions, deposits/letters of credit, minimum bills, take-or-pay style commitments, long contract terms, and termination charges sized to unrecovered project-driven costs. The exact mechanism matters less than preventing the risk from silently moving to existing customers.
What does the community receive in return? Resource use should be evaluated against benefits, not in isolation. Published estimates of tax revenue, incentives/rebates, construction employment, permanent employment and other fiscal effects. Parker currently estimates roughly $500,000–$700,000 per year in new Town revenue from this project.
What about water? “Data centers use lots of water” is not a facility-specific answer. Cooling design and actual water source/use. Parker says this facility does not use water to cool its equipment; water is limited to ordinary interior uses such as restrooms and kitchens. Upstream water used in electricity generation is a separate system-level question.
What about backup generators, noise and local air pollution? Even a low-water facility can create local externalities during outages/testing. Generator type/fuel, permitted emissions, testing schedule, expected operating hours and measured boundary noise. Parker says generators may increase noise during outages and that the project remains subject to the Town noise ordinance.
Should the county simply prohibit more data centers? That is a legitimate policy option, but it has an opportunity cost and should be compared with less restrictive guardrails. First ask whether cost allocation, water standards, emissions/noise limits, siting rules, long-term financial guarantees and load-flexibility requirements can address the actual harms. A cap/moratorium is the stronger option if those protections cannot.
SMR study: the better question is not “Are we pro- or anti-nuclear?”
Douglas County's own May 2026 direction was to develop a feasibility-study scope covering potential economic impacts, regulation, safety, federal grant opportunities and examples from other jurisdictions. By July, commissioners were seeking cost estimates for that study. The useful test is whether the eventual scope compares SMRs against credible alternatives rather than assuming an SMR is already the answer.

Colorado context: these ideas are already being debated here. Xcel filed a large-load tariff in April 2026 proposing project-specific infrastructure cost assignment, 15-year contracts, minimum payments and early-termination protections for very large loads. The PUC proceeding was active through at least June 2026 and the final approved terms may differ from Xcel's filing. None of this tariff governs Parker's CORE-served facility. Separately, a 2026 Colorado bill proposed related protections and reporting requirements for large data centers, but the bill was postponed indefinitely and did not become law.

How the 11.3 billion gallon estimate works

This is an estimate of single-family outdoor water use across the Denver Water retail service area.

  1. Start with 2024 retail treated-water sales used in this page: 48.089 billion gallons.
  2. Denver Water says 47% of retail treated-water use is by single-family customers.
  3. 48.089B × 0.47 = 22.602B gallons of estimated single-family use.
  4. Denver Water says the average single-family customer uses about 50% of household water outdoors.
  5. 22.602B × 0.50 = 11.301B gallons of estimated outdoor use.
48.089B × 0.47 × 0.50 = 11.3009B gallons/year
Important: this is not a meter reading for lawns. Outdoor water includes turf, trees, shrubs, gardens and other landscaping. The 47% and 50% figures are service-area shares/averages, so this should be read as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not an exact turf total.

How Parker's 256 GWh/year capacity scenario works

This converts published IT capacity and PUE into a full-load annual electricity scenario.

  1. Published critical IT capacity: 22.5 MW.
  2. Published PUE: 1.3. PUE means total facility power divided by IT power.
  3. 22.5 MW × 1.3 = 29.25 MW of total facility demand at full IT load.
  4. There are 8,760 hours in a non-leap year.
  5. 29.25 MW × 8,760 h = 256,230 MWh = 256.23 GWh.
22.5 MW × 1.3 × 8,760 h = 256.23 GWh/year
Important: this is not claimed actual consumption. It assumes the facility is at its full published IT capacity continuously for the entire year. Real use depends on occupancy, customer load, outages, maintenance and operating conditions.

How the 2.124 TWh/year metro capacity scenario works

This adds only the three large projects listed on the page and converts their published critical IT capacity into annual energy at hypothetical 100% use.

  1. Parker: 22.5 MW.
  2. Aurora: 160 MW+. The calculation uses 160 MW, so it is deliberately conservative relative to “160+”.
  3. North Denver: 60 MW.
  4. 22.5 + 160 + 60 = 242.5 MW of published critical IT capacity.
  5. 242.5 MW × 8,760 h = 2,124,300 MWh.
  6. 2,124,300 MWh ÷ 1,000 = 2.1243 TWh/year.
(22.5 + 160 + 60) MW × 8,760 h = 2.1243 TWh/year
Important: this is a capacity scenario, not an electricity forecast. It also excludes facility overhead because the three projects do not publish one common PUE assumption. Actual energy use can be materially lower than this figure.
